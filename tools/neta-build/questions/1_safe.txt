# A 1
Q: An arc flash is:
A: A burst of heat and light from current jumping through air
W: A slow leak of current through damp insulation to ground
W: A bright spark that only happens on DC battery circuits
W: The flicker a lamp makes when its circuit is overloaded
E: An arc flash can hit thousands of degrees in a split second. It burns, blinds and can throw molten metal.

Q: An arc flash label on switchgear tells you:
A: The hazard level and the protection needed
W: The install date and the last service date
W: Who to call for a repair or upgrade quote
W: The breaker sizes inside and their settings
E: Labels show the voltage, the arc flash boundary, and either the incident energy or the PPE category.

Q: A "shock hazard" means danger from:
A: Current passing through your body
W: Heat released by an arc blast
W: The pressure wave from a fault
W: Molten metal thrown by an arc
E: Shock is current through the body. Arc flash is a separate hazard with its own boundary.

Q: Which person may cross the restricted approach boundary?
A: A qualified person with proper PPE and a plan
W: Any experienced worker who stays careful
W: A trainee watching closely to learn the job
W: A visitor escorted by the site manager
E: Only qualified persons, protected against shock and working under a plan, go that close to exposed live parts.

# A 2
Q: The arc flash boundary is the distance where incident energy drops to:
A: 1.2 cal/cm²
W: 4.0 cal/cm²
W: 8.0 cal/cm²
W: 0.5 cal/cm²
E: 1.2 cal/cm² is about where bare skin would get a second-degree burn. Inside that line, arc-rated PPE is needed.

Q: For an exposed movable conductor at 480 V, the limited approach boundary is about:
A: 10 ft 0 in
W: 3 ft 6 in
W: 1 ft 0 in
W: 5 ft 0 in
E: NFPA 70E puts it at 10 ft for movable conductors (like overhead lines) and 3 ft 6 in for fixed parts at 151–750 V.

Q: For 480 V, the restricted approach boundary is about:
A: 1 ft 0 in
W: 3 ft 6 in
W: 10 ft 0 in
W: 0 ft 6 in
E: In the 151–750 V range, NFPA 70E sets restricted approach at 1 ft.

Q: Incident energy on an arc flash study is given in:
A: cal/cm²
W: V/m
W: A/s
W: Ω/ft
E: Calories per square centimeter at a stated working distance, usually 18 in for low-voltage gear.

# A 3
Q: Arc flash energy rises the most when:
A: Fault current is high and clearing time is long
W: The enclosure is small and painted a dark color
W: The breaker has a low amp rating and new contacts
W: The room is cool, dry and well lit for the work
E: Energy grows with current and with how long the arc lasts. Slow protection is often the bigger driver.

Q: A maintenance switch that temporarily speeds up breaker tripping is used to:
A: Lower incident energy while people work on the gear
W: Raise the breaker's continuous amp rating for a while
W: Block the breaker from tripping during maintenance
W: Let the crew test the CT ratio without opening covers
E: Faster clearing means less arc energy. It must be turned off afterward so coordination returns.

Q: An arc flash risk assessment should be reviewed at least every:
A: 5 years, or when the system changes
W: 10 years, unless an incident happens
W: Year, even when nothing has changed
W: Time a new worker joins the crew
E: NFPA 70E calls for review at intervals of no more than 5 years, and any time a change could affect the results.

Q: Why can a smaller upstream fault current sometimes make arc flash worse?
A: The breaker may trip slower on its time curve
W: Smaller fault currents make hotter, tighter arcs
W: Lower fault current raises the system voltage
W: The CTs stop reading below a certain current
E: Lower current can fall below instantaneous pickup, so the device waits on its time-delay curve and the arc lasts longer.
