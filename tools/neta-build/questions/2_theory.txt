# T 1
Q: In a three-phase system, the phases are spaced:
A: 120° apart
W: 90° apart
W: 180° apart
W: 60° apart
E: Three waves, each a third of a cycle (120°) behind the last.

Q: A "480Y/277 V" system means:
A: 480 V phase-to-phase and 277 V phase-to-neutral
W: 480 V on two phases and 277 V on the third phase
W: 480 V phase-to-ground and 277 V phase-to-phase
W: 480 V on the primary and 277 V on the secondary
E: The Y means wye. Line voltage is √3 times the phase-to-neutral voltage.

Q: In the US, normal power frequency is:
A: 60 Hz
W: 50 Hz
W: 120 Hz
W: 400 Hz
E: Most of Europe and Asia use 50 Hz. 400 Hz shows up in aircraft.

Q: Power factor compares:
A: Real power to apparent power
W: Line voltage to line current
W: Load current to fault current
W: Line voltage to phase voltage
E: PF = kW ÷ kVA. A PF of 1.0 means all the power does real work.

# T 2
Q: In a wye system, line voltage equals:
A: √3 × phase voltage
W: 1 × phase voltage
W: 2 × phase voltage
W: Phase voltage ÷ √3
E: 277 × 1.732 ≈ 480. In wye, line and phase currents are equal.

Q: In a delta system, line current equals:
A: √3 × winding current
W: 1 × winding current
W: 3 × winding current
W: Winding current ÷ √3
E: In delta, line and phase voltages are equal, but line current is √3 times winding current.

Q: Three-phase real power is:
A: √3 × V(line) × I(line) × PF
W: 3 × V(line) × I(line) × PF
W: √3 × V(line) × I(line) ÷ PF
W: V(line) × I(line) × PF × 2
E: The √3 factor comes from using line values instead of phase values.

Q: Reactive power (kVAR) is caused mainly by:
A: Inductive and capacitive loads
W: Resistive heaters and lighting
W: Ground faults on the system
W: Loose bolted connections
E: Motors and transformers draw magnetizing (inductive) current. Capacitors supply reactive power back.

# T 3
Q: kVA² equals:
A: kW² + kVAR²
W: kW + kVAR
W: kW × kVAR
W: kW² − kVAR²
E: The power triangle: kW and kVAR are the legs, kVA is the long side.

Q: Phase rotation (ABC vs ACB) matters most because it:
A: Sets which way three-phase motors spin
W: Sets the system's nominal voltage level
W: Changes the frequency the loads see
W: Decides the system's power factor
E: Swap any two phases and a motor reverses. Always check rotation before energizing motor loads.

Q: Voltage unbalance is calculated as:
A: Max deviation from average ÷ average × 100
W: Highest phase voltage − lowest phase voltage
W: Average voltage ÷ highest voltage × 100
W: Line voltage ÷ phase voltage × 100
E: Example: 470, 480, 490 V → average 480, max deviation 10 → about 2.1%.

Q: A small voltage unbalance makes a motor's current unbalance:
A: Several times larger
W: About the same size
W: Somewhat smaller
W: Drop to zero
E: Roughly 6–10 times the voltage unbalance, which overheats windings fast.

# T 4
Q: Negative-sequence current in a motor causes:
A: Extra rotor heating from a field spinning backward
W: Faster spinning and higher output torque
W: Lower winding temperature at full load
W: A better power factor at light loads
E: Negative sequence creates a counter-rotating field that induces high-frequency rotor currents and heat.

Q: The three symmetrical components are:
A: Positive, negative and zero sequence
W: Line, phase and neutral sequence
W: Real, reactive and apparent power
W: Primary, secondary and tertiary
E: Any unbalanced set can be broken into these three balanced sets. Faults are analyzed this way.

Q: Zero-sequence current shows up mainly during:
A: Ground faults
W: Balanced three-phase load
W: Ungrounded phase-to-phase faults
W: Normal motor starting
E: 3I0 = Ia + Ib + Ic. It's zero when the phases balance and appears when current returns through ground.

Q: Harmonics from VFDs and electronics mostly cause:
A: Extra heating in neutrals and transformers
W: A drop in the system's base frequency
W: A better displacement power factor
W: Slower relay trips on every feeder
E: Triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th...) add up in the neutral. K-rated transformers handle the extra heat.

# W 1
Q: A one-line (single-line) diagram shows:
A: The power system with one line for all phases
W: Every control wire and terminal number
W: The building floor plan and room layout
W: One phase of the system in full detail
E: It's the map of sources, transformers, breakers and loads. Start every job here.

Q: A schematic (elementary) diagram is best for:
A: Following control logic and trip circuits
W: Seeing where gear sits in the room
W: Ordering replacement parts by number
W: Showing cable lengths and routes
E: Schematics show how contacts, coils and relays work together, not where they physically are.

Q: A wiring diagram shows:
A: Actual terminal-to-terminal connections
W: The protection settings for each relay
W: The utility service and its metering
W: The building's load schedule by panel
E: Use it when you need to find the physical wire on a terminal strip.

Q: On a drawing, "52" next to a breaker symbol means:
A: An AC circuit breaker
W: A 52-amp breaker rating
W: Breaker number 52 on site
W: A 52 kV voltage rating
E: ANSI device number 52 is an AC circuit breaker. 52a and 52b are its auxiliary contacts.

# W 2
Q: A 52a contact is:
A: Open when the breaker is open, closed when it's closed
W: Closed when the breaker is open, open when it's closed
W: Always closed, unless the breaker is racked fully out
W: The trip coil that opens the breaker on a fault
E: "a" follows the breaker. "b" is the opposite.

Q: A three-line diagram adds what a one-line hides:
A: Each phase, CT polarity and relay wiring
W: The building's square footage and rooms
W: Purchase prices for each piece of gear
W: Fewer details, for quick overview use
E: Three-lines are needed to check CT polarity and how relays and meters are wired.

Q: Drawings normally show devices in their:
A: De-energized (shelf) state
W: Energized, in-service state
W: Tripped state after a fault
W: Most common operating state
E: A "normally open" contact is drawn open, even if it's usually closed in service.

Q: Before testing, a field drawing set should be checked for:
A: The latest revision and field markups
W: The cleanest, easiest-to-read copy
W: The original issue for construction
W: The title block and page count only
E: Old drawings cause wrong-device testing and missed sources. Red-line any differences you find.

# W 3
Q: A polarity dot on a CT on a three-line drawing shows:
A: Which way current enters for in-phase output
W: Which secondary tap sets the CT's ratio
W: Where the CT secondary gets grounded
W: The CT's accuracy class for relaying
E: Current into the dotted primary side comes out of the dotted secondary side. Relays depend on this.

Q: An "X" in a transformer terminal label (X1, X2) usually marks:
A: The low-voltage side
W: The high-voltage side
W: The neutral terminal only
W: The tertiary winding only
E: H terminals are high voltage, X terminals are low voltage.

Q: As-built drawings are:
A: Updated to show what was actually installed
W: The original design drawings from the engineer
W: Rough sketches made for bidding the project
W: Renderings made for the owner's marketing
E: Field changes get red-lined, then drafted into as-builts at turnover.

Q: A breaker trip circuit on a schematic runs from DC through:
A: The relay trip contact, the 52a contact and the trip coil
W: The relay trip contact, the 52b contact and the close coil
W: The CT secondary, the relay and back to the CT ground
W: The PT secondary, the meter and back to the PT ground
E: The 52a opens the circuit once the breaker trips, which protects the trip coil.

# W 4
Q: As-found CT wiring disagrees with the three-line. The right first move:
A: Document it and confirm with the engineer before changing anything
W: Rewire it to match the drawing right away, since drawings govern
W: Leave it as is, since the relays haven't caused any false trips
W: Mark up the drawing to match the field and keep testing
E: Either could be wrong. Changing wiring blindly can make protection operate backward.

Q: A trip coil keeps burning out. On the schematic you suspect:
A: A missing or failed 52a contact in series with the coil
W: A blown indicating lamp on the breaker control panel
W: An extra ground on the CT secondary circuit
W: A mislabeled breaker on the front of the lineup
E: Trip coils are short-duty. Without the 52a breaking the circuit, they stay energized and fail.

Q: On a protection one-line, "87T" over a transformer zone means:
A: Transformer differential protection
W: A transformer rated for 87 kVA
W: The 87th transformer on the site
W: A transformer alarm at 87 °C
E: 87 is differential. The T suffix says it protects the transformer.

Q: A lockout relay (86) contact appears in a breaker's close circuit. Its purpose:
A: Block reclosing until someone resets it
W: Speed up the breaker's closing time
W: Bypass the trip circuit during testing
W: Measure the breaker's closing time
E: After a serious fault, the 86 prevents closing back in until the fault is found and the relay reset.

# M 1
Q: Ohm's law says voltage equals:
A: Current × resistance
W: Current ÷ resistance
W: Resistance ÷ current
W: Current + resistance
E: V = I × R. Rearrange for I = V ÷ R or R = V ÷ I.

Q: 1 megohm (MΩ) equals:
A: 1,000,000 ohms
W: 1,000 ohms
W: 100,000 ohms
W: 0.001 ohms
E: Insulation readings are in MΩ or GΩ. 1 GΩ = 1,000 MΩ.

Q: 1 micro-ohm (µΩ) equals:
A: 0.000001 ohm
W: 0.001 ohm
W: 1,000 ohms
W: 0.0001 ohm
E: Contact resistance on breakers is measured in µΩ, millionths of an ohm.

Q: 1 kV equals:
A: 1,000 volts
W: 100 volts
W: 10,000 volts
W: 1,000,000 volts
E: A 13.8 kV feeder runs at 13,800 volts.

# M 2
Q: Full-load current of a 1,000 kVA, 480 V three-phase transformer is about:
A: 1,203 A
W: 2,083 A
W: 694 A
W: 3,610 A
E: I = kVA × 1000 ÷ (1.732 × V) = 1,000,000 ÷ 831 ≈ 1,203 A.

Q: To convert 68 °F to Celsius:
A: (68 − 32) × 5/9 = 20 °C
W: (68 − 32) × 9/5 = 65 °C
W: 68 × 5/9 = 38 °C
W: (68 + 32) × 5/9 = 56 °C
E: Temperature matters because insulation readings are corrected to a standard temperature.

Q: A 50 hp motor at 460 V draws about 65 A. A 100 hp motor at the same voltage draws about:
A: 124 A
W: 65 A
W: 96 A
W: 250 A
E: Current scales roughly with horsepower at the same voltage. Use the NEC table or nameplate for exact values.

Q: Power in a resistor carrying 10 A through 5 Ω is:
A: 500 W
W: 50 W
W: 250 W
W: 2 W
E: P = I²R = 100 × 5 = 500 W.

# M 3
Q: Available fault current at a transformer secondary is roughly:
A: Full-load amps ÷ %Z (as a decimal)
W: Full-load amps × %Z (as a decimal)
W: Secondary voltage ÷ transformer kVA
W: Full-load amps × the turns ratio
E: 1,203 A ÷ 0.0575 ≈ 20,900 A for a 1,000 kVA, 5.75%Z transformer (infinite source).

Q: A 13,800 V to 480 V transformer has a voltage ratio of:
A: 28.75 : 1
W: 13.8 : 1
W: 48 : 1
W: 2.875 : 1
E: 13,800 ÷ 480 = 28.75. TTR testers compare measured ratio to this.

Q: A 1200:5 CT carrying 600 A primary gives a secondary current of:
A: 2.5 A
W: 5 A
W: 1.25 A
W: 10 A
E: Ratio is 240:1. 600 ÷ 240 = 2.5 A.

Q: A PT with ratio 4200:120 reads 118 V secondary. Primary voltage is about:
A: 4,130 V
W: 4,200 V
W: 3,500 V
W: 4,800 V
E: Ratio is 35:1. 118 × 35 = 4,130 V.

# M 4
Q: Insulation resistance roughly halves for every:
A: 10 °C rise in temperature
W: 1 °C rise in temperature
W: 50 °C rise in temperature
W: 10 °C drop in temperature
E: That's why readings are temperature-corrected to a base (often 20 °C, or 40 °C for rotating machines).

Q: A cable reads 2,000 MΩ at 30 °C. Corrected to 20 °C (halving per 10 °C), it's about:
A: 4,000 MΩ
W: 1,000 MΩ
W: 2,000 MΩ
W: 8,000 MΩ
E: Colder insulation reads higher, so correcting down 10 °C doubles the value.

Q: A 13,800 V delta to 480Y/277 transformer is TTR-tested per phase: HV winding vs LV phase-to-neutral winding. Expected ratio:
A: About 49.8
W: About 28.75
W: About 16.6
W: About 86.3
E: Each HV winding sees 13,800 V; the LV winding it links sees 277 V. 13,800 ÷ 277 ≈ 49.8.

Q: A motor's voltages read 455, 470, 475 V. Voltage unbalance is about:
A: 2.5%
W: 4.3%
W: 0.5%
W: 20%
E: Average 466.7. Biggest deviation is 11.7. 11.7 ÷ 466.7 ≈ 2.5%. Above about 1%, NEMA suggests derating.

# F 1
Q: A "fault" in a power system is:
A: An abnormal current path, like a short circuit
W: A planned shutdown for maintenance work
W: A low power factor on a large motor load
W: A breaker carrying its full rated load
E: Faults can be phase-to-phase, phase-to-ground, or three-phase, and can carry huge current.

Q: The most common type of fault on power systems is:
A: Single line-to-ground
W: Three-phase bolted
W: Phase-to-phase only
W: Open neutral
E: Most faults start as one conductor touching ground through insulation failure or contact.

Q: Equipment grounding conductors are there to:
A: Give fault current a low-impedance path to the source
W: Carry the normal load current back to the source
W: Raise the voltage on metal parts during faults
W: Serve as the neutral for single-phase loads
E: A good path lets the breaker trip fast and keeps metal enclosures near ground potential.

Q: Bonding means:
A: Connecting metal parts so they're at the same potential
W: Gluing insulation sleeves in place on the bus bars
W: Tying every neutral to ground in each downstream panel
W: Tightening all bus bolts to the maker's torque value
E: Bonded parts can't have dangerous voltage between them.

# F 2
Q: A solidly grounded wye system on a ground fault will:
A: Draw high fault current and trip quickly
W: Keep running and alarm, with no trip
W: Draw only a few amps of fault current
W: Shift the frequency until it trips
E: Low impedance to ground means large fault current. Protection clears it fast.

Q: A high-resistance grounded (HRG) system limits ground fault current to:
A: A few amps, so it can keep running and alarm
W: Thousands of amps, so the breaker trips fast
W: Zero amps, so a ground fault does nothing
W: Exactly the load current of the faulted feeder
E: HRG keeps processes running on a first ground fault while crews locate it.

Q: The neutral-to-ground bond should be made:
A: At the service or the separately derived source only
W: In every panel so each one has its own good ground
W: At every receptacle to keep the neutral at zero volts
W: At the farthest panel from the service entrance
E: Extra bonds downstream put neutral current on ground paths and confuse ground fault protection.

Q: An ungrounded delta system's big risk on a first ground fault is:
A: Transient overvoltages that stress insulation
W: An immediate high-current trip of the main
W: Lower voltage on all three phases at once
W: Instant reversal of every motor on the bus
E: Intermittent arcing grounds can build voltage to several times normal. Ground detectors help find faults.

# F 3
Q: A low-resistance grounded system typically limits ground fault current to:
A: Hundreds of amps, and trips
W: Under 10 amps, and only alarms
W: Zero amps, so nothing happens
W: Full bolted three-phase fault level
E: Common on medium-voltage systems: limits damage while still giving enough current for relays.

Q: A bolted three-phase fault usually gives:
A: The highest fault current, used for equipment ratings
W: The lowest fault current, used for relay pickup
W: No current, since the phases cancel each other
W: Only negative-sequence current in the system
E: Equipment interrupting and withstand ratings are checked against it.

Q: The X/R ratio of the fault path matters because it sets:
A: How much DC offset (asymmetry) the fault current has
W: The nominal system voltage at the faulted bus
W: The phase rotation seen by downstream motors
W: The ground conductor size needed by the NEC
E: High X/R means more asymmetry, which raises peak current that breakers must handle.

Q: Why is a ground fault on an HRG system urgent even if nothing trips?
A: A second fault on another phase becomes phase-to-phase
W: HRG systems can't alarm, so nobody will notice it
W: The ground fault changes the system frequency
W: The ground fault lowers the system power factor
E: Find and fix the first fault before a second one causes a high-current trip.

# F 4
Q: A ground fault relay never trips during a real ground fault. A likely cause:
A: A downstream neutral-ground bond gives current another path
W: The fault current was far too large for the relay to see
W: The relay's pickup was set much lower than the study
W: The CT ratio was too small for the ground fault level
E: Current returning on the ground path outside the sensor's window makes the fault look like balanced load.

Q: A zero-sequence (window) CT around all phases and the neutral reads:
A: Only the current not returning through those conductors
W: The total load current of the circuit it surrounds
W: The current on phase A only, for metering use
W: The phase-to-ground voltage of the circuit
E: Balanced current cancels. What's left is current leaving through ground, the ground fault.

Q: An HRG resistor reads open during testing. The system has become:
A: Effectively ungrounded, with no ground fault detection
W: Solidly grounded, with high ground fault current
W: Better protected, since current is now zero
W: Unaffected, since the resistor is only a sensor
E: An open resistor loses fault detection and lets overvoltages build. It's a priority fix.

Q: Fault current split between two parallel ground paths risks that:
A: Neither path carries enough to trip its own sensor
W: The fault clears faster than normal on both paths
W: Voltage drops to zero everywhere on the system
W: Breaker interrupting ratings increase as a result
E: Divided current can fall below pickup. Coordinate grounding design with relay CT locations.

# I 1
Q: Electrical insulation's job is to:
A: Keep current in the conductor and out of other paths
W: Let current flow faster through the conductor
W: Store energy in the cable for short outages
W: Measure the voltage on the conductor
E: Good insulation has very high resistance. Testing checks it hasn't broken down.

Q: Insulation breaks down over time mainly from:
A: Heat, moisture, contamination and electrical stress
W: Running too lightly loaded for long periods
W: Normal room lighting and fresh paint over time
W: Being stored in a clean, dry warehouse
E: These stresses age insulation. Testing tracks the trend before it fails.

Q: Higher insulation resistance generally means:
A: Better insulation condition
W: Worse insulation condition
W: The equipment is energized
W: The meter needs calibration
E: Low or falling readings point to moisture, dirt or damage.

Q: Dust and moisture on insulator surfaces cause:
A: Surface leakage current
W: Higher insulation readings
W: Lower load current
W: Faster breaker trips
E: Cleaning and drying often bring readings back up. The guard terminal can separate surface leakage.

# I 2
Q: When DC test voltage is applied to insulation, the total current is made of:
A: Capacitive charging, absorption and leakage currents
W: Load, fault and neutral currents added together
W: Positive, negative and zero sequence currents
W: Leakage current alone, with nothing else present
E: Charging dies fast, absorption fades over minutes, and leakage stays. Good insulation has low leakage.

Q: The polarization index (PI) is the ratio of:
A: 10-minute reading ÷ 1-minute reading
W: 1-minute reading ÷ 10-minute reading
W: 60-second reading ÷ 30-second reading
W: Test voltage ÷ leakage current at 1 min
E: Good insulation keeps absorbing, so resistance rises over time and PI is higher.

Q: The dielectric absorption ratio (DAR) usually compares:
A: 60-second ÷ 30-second readings
W: 10-minute ÷ 1-minute readings
W: 30-second ÷ 60-second readings
W: 15-second ÷ 60-second readings
E: DAR is a quicker version of PI, used when a 10-minute test isn't practical.

Q: A PI near 1.0 usually means:
A: Insulation is wet, dirty or deteriorated
W: Insulation is clean, dry and excellent
W: The megohmmeter's battery is failing
W: The test ran longer than it should
E: If resistance doesn't rise with time, leakage is dominating. Many machines want PI ≥ 2.0.

# I 3
Q: Dissipation factor (tan delta) measures:
A: How much test current is lost as heat in the insulation
W: The cable's total length from end to end
W: Voltage drop along the cable under full load
W: How fast the breaker opens on a fault
E: Perfect insulation would be purely capacitive. A rising loss angle signals aging or moisture.

Q: Partial discharge is:
A: Small sparks inside voids or on insulation surfaces
W: Normal load sharing between parallel cables
W: A breaker opening part way and then closing
W: Static on the test leads, not the insulation
E: PD eats insulation from the inside over time and is a leading cause of MV failures.

Q: Why are insulation readings corrected to a base temperature?
A: Resistance changes a lot with temperature, so trends need a common base
W: Megohmmeters only work properly when the gear is at exactly 20 °C
W: Correcting the readings makes marginal equipment pass more often
W: It's only needed on DC equipment, since AC gear isn't affected
E: Without correction, a cold day looks like improvement and a hot day like a failure.

Q: For a machine whose 1-minute IR is above 5,000 MΩ, PI is:
A: Not a reliable indicator
W: Always required to be 4.0
W: Automatically a failing result
W: The only result that matters
E: IEEE 43 notes that at very high values the PI may not be meaningful. Judge by the absolute value and trend.

# I 4
Q: One phase of a cable shows much higher dissipation factor than the others. Most likely:
A: Localized aging or water trees on that phase
W: Normal behavior for one phase in every cable
W: That phase is in better shape than the others
W: The test set was on the wrong frequency
E: Comparing phases of the same cable run is one of the strongest tools for spotting a weak one.

Q: Tip-up in a power factor test means:
A: PF rising with test voltage, a sign of voids
W: PF dropping as insulation temperature rises
W: The bushing's test tap cap working loose
W: Current dropping as test voltage rises
E: Good insulation's PF is fairly flat with voltage. A rise points to internal discharges.

Q: Water trees in XLPE cable grow fastest with:
A: Moisture plus electrical stress over time
W: Dry heat and no electrical stress at all
W: Low DC voltage and dry soil conditions
W: Cold temperatures alone, with no moisture
E: Water trees lower breakdown strength and can turn into electrical trees that fail the cable.

Q: Why can high DC test voltage harm aged XLPE cable?
A: It can trap space charge that stresses weak spots later
W: It overheats the copper conductor during the test
W: It changes the insulation's color and its rating
W: It shrinks the outer jacket away from the shield
